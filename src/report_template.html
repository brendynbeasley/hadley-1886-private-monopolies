Hadley, "Private Monopolies and Public Rights," QJE vol. 1 no. 1, October 1886

Was Hadley right about railroads? A test with modern company data

Hadley argued that industries with heavy fixed costs cannot sustain competition. Rate wars end in merger, and prices then recover. This page tests that claim on 20 real US transport companies (322 firm-years of SEC filings, fiscal 2009 to 2025) on federal price indexes going back to 1969, and on historical railroad records that start in 1830.

  • 33% vs 9% vs 4%Average operating margin: railroads, truckers, airlines. Railroads own their track. The other two run on public infrastructure.
  • 10.6% vs 10.6%Operating return on assets, rail vs trucking. Once the cost of the track is counted, the rail advantage is gone.
  • 4.8%Airline return on assets. Hadley's "ruinous competition" case: falling fares, bankruptcies, then mergers.
  • −31%, then +27%Real rail rates fell from 1981 to 2001 while the industry consolidated, and have risen since.

Methodology breakdown

Hadley's 1886 article is a historical and analytical argument. It has no sample, no variables and no statistical test. The breakdown below therefore describes the empirical study on this page, which was built to test his central claim. Anything Hadley's text does not supply, or that I chose by convention, is tagged Inferred/Recommended.

Section 1: Study architecture and variable logic

Research goal. To test whether firms that own their own infrastructure (Hadley's "highway and carrier under one head") earn higher and steadier profits than firms that compete on public infrastructure, and whether any difference survives once capital intensity is accounted for.

Target population. Large US freight and passenger carriers in rail, trucking and air transport. By extension, firms in high-fixed-cost network industries. Inferred/Recommended

Sample and sampling framework. n = 322 firm-year observations from 20 firms, fiscal 2009 to 2025 (rail 5 firms, 81 obs; trucking 9 firms, 139 obs; air 6 firms, 102 obs). The panel is unbalanced. Sampling was purposive (non-probability): every US Class I railroad with its own SEC filings, the six largest US passenger airlines, and nine large publicly listed truckload and less-than-truckload carriers. Supplementary time-series samples from FRED: 57 annual price observations (1969 to 2025), 91 annual freight-rate observations (1865 to 1955), 54 annual industry margins (1908 to 1961) and 123 annual construction counts (1830 to 1952). These are complete national series, not samples drawn from a larger set.

Section 2: Formal hypotheses and inference logic

Statistical hypotheses. Let βRail be the population difference in mean operating margin between rail and trucking firms, holding year constant.

  • H0: βRail = 0. Equivalently, in the firm-level test, μRail = μTruck = μAir.
  • Ha: βRail ≠ 0. At least one mode mean differs.
  • Secondary: the same pair of hypotheses with return on assets as the DV.

Directionality. Two-tailed. Hadley's theory predicts a sign (rail higher), which would permit a one-tailed test, but the two-tailed test is the conservative convention and leaves room to detect an effect in the unexpected direction. Inferred/Recommended

Type I error. α = 0.05 Inferred/Recommended. A Type I error here would mean concluding that owning the infrastructure raises margins when the gap in this sample arose by chance. In practice it would lend false support to the case for tighter rail rate regulation.

Type II error and power. A Type II error would mean missing a real profitability difference, for example concluding that railroads earn no excess return on assets when they do. With only 20 firms (5 rail against 15 others), a firm-level two-sample test reaches power of 0.80 only for very large effects, roughly Cohen's d ≥ 1.5. A conventional large effect (d = 0.8) would need about 26 firms per group. The margin effect observed (d ≈ 6) is far above that threshold. The return-on-assets test is not: its minimum detectable difference at 80% power is about 7 percentage points, so the null result there rules out a large rail premium but cannot rule out a modest one.

Section 3: Estimation and mathematical modelling

Primary test. Multiple linear regression (pooled OLS with year fixed effects and standard errors clustered by firm). The DV is ratio-scaled and continuous, the IV is nominal with three levels, and the same firms are observed repeatedly, so observations within a firm are not independent. Clustering handles that dependence. Because 20 clusters is few, the result is confirmed with a one-way ANOVA and a Kruskal-Wallis test on the 20 firm-level means, where independence holds by construction.

Marginit = β0 + β1·Raili + β2·Airi + γt + εit

i = firm, t = fiscal year, γt = year fixed effects, trucking is the reference category. The extended model adds β3·ln(PP&E/Revenue)it.

Section 4: Interpretation metrics and reporting standard

Test statistic and degrees of freedom. For each coefficient, t = b ÷ SE(b), referred to a t distribution with G − 1 = 19 degrees of freedom, where G is the number of firm clusters Inferred/Recommended. For the firm-level ANOVA, F with dfbetween = k − 1 = 2 and dfwithin = N − k = 17. For Kruskal-Wallis, H is compared with χ² on k − 1 = 2 df.

Effect size. The unstandardized coefficient in percentage points is the most readable measure. Eta-squared (η²) reports the share of between-firm variance explained by mode, and Cohen's d the standardized rail-versus-others gap.

Decision rules. Reject H0 if p < 0.05, or equivalently if the 95% confidence interval for β excludes 0. Otherwise fail to reject.

Data and limits

Company data. SEC EDGAR XBRL "company facts" for Union Pacific, CSX, Norfolk Southern, BNSF, Kansas City Southern; J.B. Hunt, Knight-Swift, Werner, Old Dominion, Schneider, Heartland Express, Saia, ArcBest, Marten; Delta, United, American, Southwest, Alaska Air, JetBlue. Values are as reported in annual 10-K filings.

Price data. Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED), originally from the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Historical data. NBER Macrohistory Database series on FRED. The freight-rate line joins four series with different coverage, scaled at their overlaps, so small year-to-year moves near the joins (1882, 1888, 1920) should not be over-read. There is no public rate series on FRED between 1956 and 1968, so the historical and modern price charts are kept separate.

How far back each source goes. Railroad construction 1830, share prices 1855, freight rates 1865, industry revenue and expenses 1907, rail price index 1969, company filings 2007. The SEC's structured data starts in 2009 with two years of comparatives. Adding 2007 and 2008 leaves the rail-versus-trucking margin gap at 23.5 points. Company-level figures before that exist in CRSP and Compustat (from 1926 and 1950), which need WRDS access.

Not used. Wharton's WRDS (Compustat, CRSP) requires an institutional subscription and was not used, so every input here is freely downloadable. The SEC filings are the same underlying source Compustat draws on, so the figures should match closely if you re-run this in WRDS.

Limits. Twenty firms is a small sample and was not drawn at random. Operating margin and book return on assets are accounting measures, not economic profit. Canadian National and CPKC are excluded because they report under different filings. Airline assets exclude some leased aircraft. Background facts on the Staggers Act, the count of large railroads and the pending Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern merger come from Railway Age, Logistics Management and Supply Chain 24/7.
