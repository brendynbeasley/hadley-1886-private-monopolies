# Private Monopolies, Then and Now

*A reading of Arthur T. Hadley, "Private Monopolies and Public Rights," Quarterly Journal of Economics 1, no. 1 (1886): 28–44.*

Arthur Hadley published this essay in October 1886, in the very first issue of the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. Congress passed the Interstate Commerce Act four months later. He was writing about railroads, but his real subject was any business where most of the cost is sunk before the first customer shows up. I wanted to know whether his argument still holds when you check it against numbers he never had, so I pulled railroad statistics that reach back to 1830, federal price indexes from 1969 onward, and seventeen years of audited filings for twenty American transport companies.

His argument runs like this. A railroad's costs are mostly fixed: track, bridges, interest on the debt. Hauling one more carload costs almost nothing. So when two railroads fight over the same traffic, each will keep cutting its rate as long as the rate covers the fuel and the crew, even if nothing is left over for the track. Hadley compares this to a farmer who sells wheat below the cost of his rent because the rent is owed either way. Nobody can stop first, because whoever stops still owes the fixed charges while the rival takes the traffic. The fight ends in bankruptcy or merger, and after the merger prices return to "a non-competitive figure." His conclusion was unpopular then and is still uncomfortable. Forcing competition on such an industry does not protect customers. He thought it better to admit the monopoly and regulate it through published accounts and equal treatment of shippers.

I started with his own century. The National Bureau of Economic Research compiled railroad records long ago, and they are kind to him. Track building peaked in 1872, 1882 and 1887. Railroad share prices fell 48 percent between 1872 and 1877 and 35 percent between 1881 and 1885, and the panic of 1857 took 40 percent. Those are the three crises he lists. Meanwhile the charge for moving a ton of freight one mile on thirteen large roads dropped from 3.1 cents in 1865 to 0.8 cents in 1890. Adjusted for the general price level, shippers in 1886 paid less than half of what they had paid at the end of the Civil War. Hadley wrote that competition "had produced rapid development and low average charges" while ruining investors, and both halves of that sentence check out.

One thing did not check out. I tried to catch his cycle in the yearly numbers by asking whether a heavy year of construction was followed by a sharper fall in rates. It was not. Rates fell in 30 of the 35 years after 1865 whether or not anyone was building, so the crises show up in share prices and I could not find them in the average rate.

What came next is the outcome Hadley feared. He warned that rates fixed too low by a public authority would "check railroad development." In 1906 the Hepburn Act gave the Interstate Commerce Commission power to set maximum rates. From 1908 to 1916 the large railroads kept 31.5 cents of each revenue dollar as operating profit. From 1921 to 1961, leaving out the Second World War, they kept 23.6 cents, and the drop is statistically significant. Track mileage doubled between 1890 and 1916 and was no higher in 1942 than in 1916. Trucks and cars arrived in those same decades, so I would not pin all of that on the regulators. Still, it is the pattern he predicted.

Hadley also gave me a ready-made experiment for the present day, though he did not call it one. He points out that the old law kept the highway public and let private carriers compete on it, and that this worked well as long as anyone could put a wagon on the road. The railroad broke the arrangement because it was highway and carrier at once. Today we have all three cases side by side. Truckers run on public interstates. Airlines fly through public airspace and land at public airports. Freight railroads still own every mile of their own track.

The filings sort themselves into those groups almost too neatly. From 2009 to 2025 the five big railroads in my sample kept about 33 cents of every revenue dollar as operating profit, which is back where the industry stood before 1917. The nine trucking companies kept 9 cents and the six airlines kept 4. The least profitable railroad in the group averaged 31 percent. The most profitable trucker, Old Dominion, averaged 19. The gap between rail and trucking is 24 points after adjusting for the year, and the confidence interval runs from 20 to 28. Results this clean are rare in company data.

It would be easy to stop there and declare the railroads guilty. I think that would misread both Hadley and the data. A railroad needs about $2.57 of plant and equipment to produce a dollar of revenue. A trucker needs 54 cents. A big margin on a huge asset base can still be an ordinary return, so I divided operating profit by total assets instead of by revenue. The rail advantage disappears. Railroads and truckers each averaged between 10 and 11 percent on assets, and the difference is nowhere near statistical significance. The 33-cent margin is what it costs to keep paying for the track. Hadley's "fixed charges" are sitting right there in the income statement, getting covered.

The airlines are the other half of his prediction, and the sadder half. They earned under 5 percent on assets, about half of what the others did, and the gap is significant. Fares fell in eleven of the last twenty-one years. Adjusted for inflation, a ticket in 2025 cost about a quarter less than in 1989. That is wonderful if you are a passenger. For the companies it meant a decade of bankruptcies (United, Delta, Northwest, American) followed by mergers that left four carriers with most of the domestic market. Hadley described that exact sequence 140 years ago, down to the order of events.

The price history of the railroads fits him as well, with one twist. After the Staggers Act loosened rate regulation in 1980, inflation-adjusted rail rates fell by about 31 percent between 1981 and 2001. During those same years the industry went from more than thirty large railroads to seven. Then the direction reversed. Since 2001 real rail rates have climbed 27 percent, and since 2004 rail prices have doubled while trucking rates and consumer prices rose about 70 to 75 percent. The data shows both phases Hadley described, low rates while the fight lasted and restored prices after the mergers ended it.

I should be straight about where the evidence is thin. Hadley claimed that competition makes prices and profits unstable, and at first the airlines seem to prove it, since their margins swing three times as widely as anyone else's. But almost all of that comes from 2020. Drop the pandemic year and the difference in volatility is no longer significant. Rail prices do look steadier than truck rates or airfares, but with twenty-one annual observations the test cannot confirm it. So I would call that part of his argument plausible and unproven. My sample is also twenty firms chosen because they file with the SEC, which means I can describe these companies well and should be careful about anything broader. And equal returns on assets do not prove that rail prices are fair, because book assets are an accounting number and the railroads' captive shippers would have plenty to say about their own rates.

Still, I came away thinking Hadley got the hard part right. He understood that the question to ask of a high-fixed-cost industry is what kind of monopoly you will end up with and who gets to see its books. That question is live again. Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern have asked the Surface Transportation Board to approve an $85 billion merger that would create the first coast-to-coast railroad, and the Board has spent 2026 demanding more information about market shares before it rules. Hadley would have recognized every step of that proceeding. He would probably have told the Board what he told his readers in 1886, that a strong road is easier to hold responsible than a weak one, as long as it is made to show its accounts.
